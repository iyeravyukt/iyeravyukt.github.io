TEND: Cartesian Gripper with Tactile & Vision Assistance

Mechanism design · Assistive robotics

Nov 2026 · Healthcare trackMakeCU 2026, Columbia University

My roleMechanical Designer, team of three

A fixed-mount, four-finger robotic hand whose fingers each slide independently, so it can push, pull, twist, squeeze, and hold. Built in 24 hours to open pill bottles and caps for people with a weak grip.

TEND prototype
Exploded CAD
Twist simulation

Problem

More than 50 million US adults have arthritis, and 23 million are limited in everyday activities because of it. In one survey of 2,245 people with rheumatic conditions, 92% struggled to open medicine packaging and 22% struggled every day. Assistive robotic arms cost around $35,000, so for many people a pill bottle means waiting for someone else to help.

How it works

A pill bottle is placed in the hand. The bottom two fingers hold it steady while the top two slide in opposite directions to twist the cap, like rolling a pencil between your palms. The hand repeats grip, twist, release, and recenter, turning the cap about 100° per stroke on a 28 mm cap.

My contribution

Rebuilt the Cartesian Hand mechanism from Duke's General Robotics Lab in Fusion 360 and modified it for TEND. Redesigned the joints around 180° servos to get the most finger travel, about 25 mm per joint. Designed a 16-tooth, module 1 herringbone rack-and-pinion for every joint (about 0.14 mm of travel per servo degree), using a test coupon to tune the press fit onto the servo spline. Iterated 17 printed parts (under 300 g of PLA) through several rounds of sanding and reprinting until every finger slid smoothly.

Result

In one weekend the team went from CAD to a sensor-integrated prototype with seven actuated motions, live current and grip sensing, a tactile sensor streaming at about 18 frames per second, and a digital twin mirroring every motion. The full twist routine ran in simulation at about 6 s per cycle, removing and lifting the cap in under 45 s. The available micro servos lacked consistent torque, so the hardware demo was hand-actuated with every sensor streaming live; stronger servos are a drop-in upgrade.

Details

  • Actuation: 4 SG90 finger-slide servos and 3 MG90S servos for the base squeeze, top squeeze, and lift, all on a PCA9685 driver.
  • Sensing: a 16×32 FlexiTac tactile array on the gripping finger, two INA219 current sensors on the squeeze servos, and a Logitech webcam.
  • Control: an ESP32-C6 hub streams data at 25 Hz to a Python control loop. Grip is current-controlled, with contact at about +40 mA, a hold near 150 mA, back-off above about 450 mA, and an automatic overload cutoff. Servos stay limp at boot until calibrated.
  • Gemini in the loop: each motion (grip, twist, release, recenter, lift, squeeze) is a function call. Gemini reads the camera and sensors, picks the next safety-checked motion, and checks that the cap is off. It never sends raw servo angles.
  • Cost: under $200 in parts, more than 25× less than the cheapest commercial robot arm.
  • Next: stronger servos so the full twist runs on hardware, then testing with people living with arthritis.
